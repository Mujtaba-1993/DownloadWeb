# Stories: Howard Marks

Built from 134 public sources (Oaktree memos 1990 to 2026, interviews and talks); see sources.md. The stories and cases he reuses, with the lesson he draws.

1. **The Nifty Fifty at Citibank (1969 to 1974).** He started as an equity analyst when the bank owned the "best" growth stocks at any price; buyers on his first day lost almost everything over five years. Lesson: price matters more than quality, and "no price too high" marks a bubble. "And if you bought the stocks the day I reported to work and held them for five years, you lost almost all your money." (S134; also S015, S020, S011)

2. **bubble.com (January 2000).** His memo on tech and internet stocks put his writing on the map; a year later the 14 stocks it named were "down 82% on average from their year-end 1999 prices and 87% from their highs in 2000." (S111) Lesson: extreme psychology plus extreme prices is the rare moment to make a call. (S114, S015, S021)

3. **Post-Lehman buying (September to December 2008).** Assuming the world would not end, Oaktree invested heavily while almost no one else would. "Bruce Karsh's team plunged in, investing an average of $400 million a week from September" (S021). In a later telling: "we put $500 million a week to work in the last 15 weeks following the bankruptcy of Lehman Brothers" (S129). Lesson: buy when the knives fall; waiting for the dust to settle means missing the bargains. (S021, S129, S073)

4. **The CIO who would not say "it can't be that bad" (October 2008).** Every scenario he answered was met with a worse one. He took that unbridled pessimism as proof that little optimism was in prices. Lesson: when no one will grant a floor, the odds favor buying. (S021, S073)

5. **The Limits to Negativism epiphany (October 2008).** Watching people only bulletproof their portfolios, he realized skepticism also means doubting the doom story. "The negative story may have looked compelling, but it's the positive story - which few believed - that held, and still holds, the greater potential for profit." (S073)

6. **"The Death of Equities" (Businessweek 1979, reread March 2012).** He missed its error in 1979 with ten years' experience, then caught the same illogic in 2012. Lesson: pattern recognition takes decades and scars. "What if the lows in optimism and enthusiasm for equities meant things couldn't get any worse?" (S021, S062)

7. **March 2020, Covid.** With the S&P 500 down a third in weeks, he advised buying some but not all, since no one knew how bad it would get. "What I would do is figure out how much you'll want to have invested by the time the bottom is reached - whenever that is - and spend part of it today." (S035, S036, S021)

8. **Bad bond issues carried to Bruce's office (2005 to 2006).** He read the market's temperature from the junk being sold. "Look at this piece of crap that got issued yesterday. If a company can raise money on this basis, there's something wrong in the market." (S138) Lesson: deal terms reveal psychology better than forecasts.

9. **Catching falling knives on Enron and 2002 distressed debt.** Oaktree averaged down as Enron bonds slid and the distressed market tanked, taking large mark-to-market losses first. "We've made a lot of money catching falling knives in the last two decades." (S079; S089)

10. **High yield bonds as shunned underdogs (late 1970s onward).** Moody's called B-rated bonds undesirable without asking about price, so holders were overpaid for the risk. "Moody's panned those bonds because they were underdogs but never asked about the price." (S037; S138)

11. **Dave, the pension fund CIO.** In 14 years his equity return never ranked above the 27th or below the 47th percentile, yet the long-run result was top tier. Lesson: avoid disasters rather than chase top-decile years. (S009; first told in S128, 1990)

12. **The six-foot man and the five-foot stream.** His favorite caution about averages: you must survive the deepest point, not the average depth. (S080, S086, S054, S037)

13. **Living with Andrew in the 2020 lockdown.** Debates with his son moved him toward valuing growth companies and long holding. "When you look at the chart for something that's gone up and to the right for 20 years, think about all the times a holder would have had to convince himself not to sell." (S020; S018)

14. **The Weimar million-mark note (October 2008).** He bought a 1,000 mark note overstamped one million marks to think about printing money's long-run cost. Lesson: rescues usually swap today's problem for a later one. (S073)

15. **Five calls in fifty years.** His son pointed out he had made only five major market calls (2000, 2004 to 2007, late 2008, 2012, March 2020). Lesson: act only at extremes. "Once in a while - once or twice a decade, perhaps - markets go so high or so low that the argument for action is compelling and the probability of being right is high." (S021)
